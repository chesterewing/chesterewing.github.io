From Data Encoder to Backend Dev · The Long Way Around

April 22, 2026 · 5 min read · career · backend · story

In December 2019 I started typing broadcast logs into spreadsheets at a GMA TV relay station. The work was straightforward: data goes in, data gets filed, data is correct or you re-enter it. I was nineteen. I thought I'd be there for six months while I figured out what came next.

I was there for four and a half years.

The slow path looked like a stuck path

I never planned to stay that long. School happened, hospital IT happened, a couple of analyst roles happened. I was working multiple jobs at once for most of that stretch, stacking experience while I tried to figure out what I actually wanted to do. From the outside it looked random. Inside, it always felt like I was reaching toward the same thing: I want to build the systems people use, not just feed them data.

The problem was that "build systems" is vague. I knew I liked code. I knew I liked hardware. I'd done a thesis project that touched both. But the path from "I can write a script" to "I can ship a backend service" isn't a straight line in the Philippines, at least not where I was. There's no obvious junior-dev pipeline waiting for you in Aparri.

What the boring jobs actually taught me

People underestimate operational work. I did. But four years of it gave me things a coding bootcamp wouldn't have:

  • Data discipline. When you've manually fixed a thousand broken records, you stop tolerating sloppy schemas. You design tables like someone might still be cleaning them in 2030, because you used to be that someone.
  • Patience for the unglamorous. Production data is messy. Backend code spends most of its life dealing with edge cases that nobody put in the requirements. After enough late nights debugging an Excel pivot, debugging an API feels almost fun.
  • User empathy at the wrong end. When you've been the one entering bad data into bad forms, you stop building bad forms.
The work that doesn't look like programming is often what makes the programming work.

The pivot, when it finally came

The shift happened in November 2025. Cebuanas Encoding Services took me on as a Backend Developer for RocketServe, a SaaS platform for process-serving agencies. My job is to build the backend behind it: APIs, auth, database, and the business-logic layer that keeps everything moving.

The first week was disorienting. Not because the code was hard, but because I was finally doing exactly the thing I'd spent five years circling. The imposter syndrome was loud. ("I'm a data person, what am I doing here?") Then I noticed I was instinctively designing tables the way I always wished previous data jobs had designed theirs. The skills had been transferring all along.

If you're on the slow path right now

If you're three years into a job that doesn't look like the career you want: first, that's not wasted time, it just doesn't feel productive yet. Second, identify what your current role is actually teaching you that the dream role couldn't. There's almost always something. Third, build small things in the gaps. RxFY (my thesis) was a real project I built while working full-time at GMA. It's still the strongest line on my resume.

The unglamorous part: there's no shortcut. You have to keep coding on the side, applying to roles that look out of reach, and pushing through the years where it feels like you're not moving. But you are moving, even when the only people who can see it are future-you and the few people you build with along the way.

Five years from data entry to backend dev. I'd do it the same way. Mostly.

If this resonated, or if you're on a similar path and want to chat, drop me a line.
